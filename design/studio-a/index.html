Studio A for TurnoutIdentity, final round

A radar with
a second life.

Turnout is not a marketing tool; it is a sense. A good bar two streets from the Garden already feels a show coming, the room goes quiet and then the pavement fills, and Turnout makes that instinct precise and gives it a voice. So the identity should feel like hospitality first and instrument second: drawn with the restraint of a good menu, with one moving part that tells you something is on its way. The previous sheets got the instrument right and stopped there. Our view is that the mark must be a radar and, in the same lines, something that belongs to the room you are trying to fill: a door, a glass, a crowd. Each idea below gives the sweep that second life.

I

Swing

The radar, drawn the way an architect draws a door.

The idea

On every floor plan a door is drawn as a hinge, a leaf and a quarter arc: that is exactly a radar sweep, so here the leading edge is the door leaf, the rings are its swing, and the afterglow is the light that spills across the threshold as it opens.

Why it is right for Turnout

Turnout sells one moment, the crowd outside walking through your door, and this mark says it in a symbol every owner has already seen on their own plans, while keeping the rings, the trail and the crisp edge you liked.

Where it fails

A quarter radar is less instantly "radar" than a full disc; it only reads as a door when the two wall stubs are there, so they can never be cropped.

II

Coupe

Half a radar hung from a line is a T. Give it a foot and it pours.

The idea

A half radar hung from a straight line is a capital T, and the same four lines with a foot become a coupe glass whose drink is the afterglow, filling behind the sweep.

Why it is right for Turnout

Three readings in four lines, the initial, the instrument and the drink the campaign actually sells, drawn in the calm grammar of a cocktail menu rather than a dashboard.

Where it fails

It says bar much louder than shop or bookshop, and at 16 px it sits close to the generic "bar" icon on maps; the radar is the third thing you see, not the first.

III

Ola

The crowd is the radar.

The idea

Thirty six ticks are the sections of a stadium bowl; the wave stands up at the leading edge and sits down behind it, amber cooling to ink like phosphor, so the afterglow is made of people showing up.

Why it is right for Turnout

It is the only mark here that literally shows turnout, it is a perfect "listening" state for an agent that watches the city, and nobody in hospitality technology owns it.

Where it fails

This is the bold one: below 24 px it collapses into a system activity spinner, and some people will read sound or loading before they read crowd.

Turnont

One thing we found and kept for the wordmark. The name does what it says: across the hinge between Turn and out, the u is an n turned half a turn around the o. In the final drawing of the wordmark we recommend cutting the u as the exact 180 degree rotation of the n (Fraunces is within a hair of it already). Nobody will notice; everyone will feel the word is balanced.

Recommendation

Swing. The door is the promise.

Swing keeps every signal you responded to, real rings, an afterglow that fades like a screen, one crisp leading edge, nothing dark or loud, and adds the one thing the radar never had: a reason to exist in hospitality. Every owner reads the door at a glance because it is on their own plans, and every customer reads it without knowing why. It survives 16 px as a door, 20 px next to the serif as a radar, and it goes on an actual door without looking like software.

Keep Ola. Not as the logo, as the motion: it is the loading and "listening" state inside the product, the thing that moves while the agent watches the city. Swing is what Turnout is; Ola is what it does. Coupe is the strongest campaign object of the three and can live in the creative templates.

The one risk

A quarter radar asks for half a second more than a full disc. If the brand is ever shown without the two wall stubs, or rotated, it stops being a door and becomes a generic fan; the walls are the idea, and they need to be protected in the guidelines from day one.
